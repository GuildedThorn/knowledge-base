# Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

## Purpose

Primordial nucleosynthesis produced the light elements in the first minutes, tightly constraining the baryon density.

## How It Works

- Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN) occurred roughly between one second and a few minutes after the Big Bang, at temperatures of about 0.1-1 MeV.
- Free protons and neutrons fused into light nuclei once the universe cooled below the deuterium binding energy.
- A deuterium bottleneck delayed synthesis: photodissociation kept deuterium scarce until the temperature dropped enough for it to survive and seed heavier nuclei.
- The neutron-to-proton ratio froze out near 1:7, largely fixing the final helium fraction.

## Key Ideas

- BBN produced helium-4 at about 25% of baryonic mass, plus trace deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7.
- Heavier elements could not form: gaps at mass 5 and mass 8 and the falling density halted the reaction chain.
- The primordial abundances depend almost entirely on the baryon-to-photon ratio, making light-element measurements a sensitive baryon-density probe.
- The BBN baryon density agrees with the independent value derived from CMB acoustic peaks, a key consistency test of standard cosmology.
- A long-standing lithium problem persists: observed lithium-7 is lower than BBN predicts.
